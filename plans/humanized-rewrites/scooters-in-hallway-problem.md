At seven-thirty in the morning, I was moving somebody's e-scooter out of the middle of the hallway. It was not leaning against a wall or tucked into a corner. It was parked dead centre, like nobody else used the floor.

In a building with older tenants, children, and people using mobility devices, that is more than an annoyance. It is a trip hazard sitting in the route everyone depends on.

My maintenance guy watched me move it and shook his head. He had been scrubbing black tire marks off the walls and floors for weeks. The rubber leaves long streaks that need the right cleaner and a lot more work than the rider probably imagines.

He told me people would keep doing it until something happened. I did not like hearing that, mostly because he might be right.

## The conversation by the elevator

Around noon, a tenant stopped me near the elevator. I am leaving out her name and floor. She walks with a cane and said a young rider had almost clipped her the week before.

She did not fall. That does not make it harmless.

I told her we were working on it. She gave me the look people give when they have heard that sentence before. We had sent letters. I had knocked on doors. I had repeated the same conversation with several households. The hallway would improve for a week and then another scooter would show up.

From her side, another notice probably looked like more paper without a result. I could not blame her for being skeptical.

The scooter problem reminds me of [why underground parking cannot become a storage room](/blog/why-your-underground-parking-garage-is-not-a-storage-unit). Different part of the property, same issue. One person's convenient storage can create a hazard for everyone else.

## Telling people no is not a storage plan

I kept coming back to the same question: if scooters cannot sit in the hallway, where should residents put them?

A secure storage area near the entrance might help. Clear signs near the elevators and stairwell doors might help too. If the safer option is also the easier option, most people will probably use it.

I was also thinking about clearer signs near the elevators and stairwell doors, along with updated lease language that spells out what happens after repeated violations. “Please be considerate” had not been enough.

## The cleaning work is real work

The tire marks may look minor beside the safety issue, but they still matter. My maintenance guy is the person on his knees with a scrub brush while the rider is upstairs and probably has no idea how much work one trip down the hall created.

Dirty, marked hallways also change how the building feels. Residents who take care of the place notice when common areas start looking neglected. Staff notice too. It gets harder to ask people to respect a space when the damage is allowed to pile up.

That is why cleanup cannot be the whole response. We can remove the marks today and have them back tomorrow if the riding continues.

## What I can do next

I do not have a perfect solution yet. I can keep the walking route clear, speak privately to the responsible households, and stop sending vague warnings that disappear into the same pile as every other notice.

I can also keep pushing for a practical storage option. Once residents have a reasonable place to leave a scooter, the individual conversations become simpler. The hallway is not storage, and there is another place to use.

If that does not change the behaviour, the next step will be formal notices. Moving one scooter while ignoring another would make the rule look optional.

I expect I will have to move another scooter before this is solved. For now, the main concern is simple: the tenant who walks with a cane should be able to reach the elevator without watching for somebody riding around the corner.
